  <h2 style="margin-top:0;">Environmental Roots of Urban Inequality</h2>
  <p>
    


My primary research investigates long-term processes of social-environmental change in cities. It responds to a structural division between urban and environmental theory: With few exceptions, research on urban environments relies on hazardous site data produced since the 1980s, severely limiting longitudinal research. In response, I develop original methods to understand industrial transitions in urban contexts and to connect environmental data with population measures at multiple spatial scales (see [this article](/publications/2021_plos/) on my novel machine learning approach to analyzing archival data, as well as [this chapter](/publications/2024_oxford/) on ML methods and large-scale data in urban research). I use these original data to conduct the first comparative analysis of social-environmental change in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, which finds that the racialization of environmental risk emerged alongside and in relation to the initial formation of segregated and unequal neighborhoods (see [this article](/publications/2026_sf/) in *Social Forces*, as well as [this article](/publications/2022_socius/) in *Socius*). This research demonstrates that interlocking social and environmental change are central to the formation of spatial inequality and that understanding industrial transitions is a necessary window into this process. Building on Du Boisian understandings of the coproduction of race and environment, my work argues that US cities must be understood through a general theory of environmental racialization, such that place stratification came to be structured through a relational process that gradually tied racial marginalization to environmental risk.

In revealing new information on large-scale environmental hazards that are absent from state and federal tracking efforts, my work additionally emphasizes the utility of computational social science to create important new data for policy research.
